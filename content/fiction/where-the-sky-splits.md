---
title: Where the Sky Splits
date: 2026-09-14
---
The communication deputy’s office was somewhere in the basement of the West Wing, a cavern beneath a splinter in the planks in the floor, a throwaway space. It felt as if it wasn’t being entirely honest. It was almost small beyond belief, almost cramped beyond being livable. This man was a professional, but he sat in the den of a minor demonic figure. The glacial walls that made up his office, bending respectfully in prayer, waited for his call to collapse and disappear the scientist that sat down before his desk. Crowded and disordered shelves jutted paper, books, and dying plants. There was a reasonable fear, palpable in the voice of the visiting man, of falling away into bushels of old papers and being forgotten along with them.

Unfittingly, the man behind the desk was in a cheery mood. Fittingly, it was clearly for show: Cordova had seen it enough to know not to be fooled, and he had seen the look come from this man in particular a whole lot. He respected it, though. He would have given up that damned smile a long time ago, were he working here. In the times Cordova had seen the man in passing in the halls, it was this look that had stuck in his head, this look he would see when he closed his eyes. Everybody working here had a look like this in their back pocket. But the man who owned this office - the man whose face and name seemed they would never really stick with the scientist no matter how he tried - he gave that look to his visitors a lot. It was really all Cordova remembered about him.

“What brings you in today?” The man’s faint grin almost made the room heavier. He sat in the secretive side of a curtain of shade made by a weak, glittering ceiling light.
The other man waited to respond, a bit confused. “I was invited here by the chief of staff, sir.”
“Sure, but what brings you in here?” The smile was suddenly absent.
“I’m sorry, sir?”
“Okay. You outrank me at the moment, so you don’t need to call me sir. Let’s just cut through that.”
“Okay.” He barely held in “sir.”
“What brings you here now?”
“Sorry, sir?”
“You’re a busy man. You’ve met with my boss, and his boss, his boss.” The chief of communications, the chief of staff, and the president; Cordova had met them in one setting or another over the last two weeks. He remembered them enough to acknowledge that this was true, but the details of each had bled into each other. Not much came to mind.
“I have, sir.”
The deputy looked defeated, and reluctantly accepted the title. “Why are you seeing me?”

To credit this man, Cordova thought, he did seem to harbor a genuine confusion, a real curiosity. The scientist waited until his words began to bubble; then boil; then they cooled, and again became cold. He had no good answer: he was shuffled into meetings often. He asked questions little, and saw little of anybody who would answer them. There was a word that described his research and its bells and whistles of secrecy quite well, but that word, too, was taboo, and would have made a jarring answer to any question. “I don’t really remember, sir.”

The deputy looked down at his hands, locked into each other by the fingers, resting on his desk. He gave an almost diplomatic answer. “Well, this meeting is on my calendar already, so let’s just get something on paper. Tell me how things have been going.”
“Things?”
“Your research, your team. What’s been going on?”
“It’s been slow, sir.”
“It’s been slow,” the deputy mirrored.
“Yes, slow.”
“What’s the issue?”
Cordova wrung his hands painfully, but was distracted from the sharp intrusion, gazing at the ceiling. He imagined he saw the sky above it. 

Cordova was a tall and perpetually out-of-place man. He shrunk in his seat, but found no extra space to hide in. He felt far too close to everything, far too enclosed. Divorced from his more useful senses and maybe miles from the light, he clawed desperately onto the remaining intellectual knowledge that he was a human, and that he was real, though he struggled to find comfort in these facts. He stood up, focused now on a small map above a bookshelf, which he was lucky enough to see from the ground, though he struggled to make out its shape. But it glistened in a trance-like way in the weak, flickering lights. To Cordova, it was the messy blob of the outstretched cosmos; to him, it was an anchor.

“Our research relies less on record than I’d like.”
“What do you mean?”
“Record. Continuity. Not a lot of historical data, not a lot of living or sane witnesses. Lots of red tape, lots of rejections, lots of theories. These theories might not work; maybe they’re equally bad. But if they all did work, that would be far worse. And that’s what it’s starting to seem like. Every theory works when you can’t talk to the person who brought it to light. Everything stays covered.”
The deputy was silent for a moment. “I’ve heard a bit about your frustration at getting witnesses.”
“I know.”
“Your visit to Montana.”
“I know.” Cordova waited. “Is that why I’m here?”
“Honestly…” The man dangled the word suspensefully in the air — it wasn’t yet decided if he was going to be honest. “I don’t know.” Cordova was suspicious, but continued. 
“Yes, Montana. And Missouri. And West Virginia. And I tried for Quebec. But I guess your people are why that one didn’t work out.” When the deputy didn’t respond, Cordova took it to mean that he wasn’t being all that honest, after all. He took it that the man knew about those trips and more.
“Montana affected you?”
“Good question.” He could hear the deputy sigh. “Does it affect you?” he volleyed back.
“That seems off-topic.”
“I don’t think it is. I think that’s the whole topic. We’re neglecting what’s right in front of us, and it makes my job almost meaningless, and that’s scary. I think the record is all we have, and I don’t think we should wait for a longer record to get wise about it. It doesn’t scare you? The idea of waking up so far from home like that? In an orchard, cold and wet? Are we going to wait for a better witness? A more jarring story? Are we going to keep putting it off until we see it up close?”
“I believe that’s the question of your research — how these things happen, am I right?”
“Well there’s the thesis. It scares me. And I don’t know how it happens. Any explanation is just as good as any other. Psychological manipulation is just as good as whatever else you read in our papers, whatever the hell those say nowadays. And it’s happening more often.” He changed his mind many times in quick succession about the nomenclature. Psychological sabotage. Meltdown. Restructuring. Parasitism. Invasion.
“That sounds premature.”
“It’s well-formed, trust me.”
“So why conclude anything if all you have is fear? It was scary when we started, it’s scary now. So there you go? Isn’t that a conclusion? Can’t we know by now whether we’re wasting our time and effort?”
Now here was a good question. At least, a question worth some hesitation. Cordova wasn’t excited about being dismissive of questions like these, but the motor of urgency that beat inside him still told him to kill any idea of simply putting things off. “The man in Montana…” Cordova said as the deputy, visible in the corner of his vision, braced his forehead with his hands. “That man stood in the orchard, looking straight up, for somewhere between 60 and 70 hours. Neck broken. Eyes dried out. Knees locked. Ankles shattered. Brain-dead. Staring up.”
Silence, for a delicate moment. “I know, Stephen.”
“Did you know all those things about him?”
“I did.”
“You knew he started waking up there weeks before? And everything he went through to get help? Did you know the Secret Service took him in for his theories and kept him there for a full day? The brain never stops moving, you know. It’s always gathering something. It’s always getting ready for something. I bet he was living out some scene where he went to go find his kids, like he said he was gonna do when he left. But he never got there.” He was exasperated and shrill. “God only knows what was going on in his head while he was there all those times, but I know it wasn’t empty. There’s a conclusion for you, and that one’s not premature.”
“Stephen. Be careful here.”
“Did you know that was the last thing he did before dying? Did you know he went straight from interrogation to the orchard, and then he was done? Because he said the word ‘alien,’ he was taken from his family, and then he was dead. But he was right. And they were told not to look for him.”
“Stephen.” Sterner this time. Definitive. Cordova stopped talking. He remained fixed on the nebulous figure, the blob in the frame, and wondered how he knew its shape.
“I don’t know how it happens,” he whispered to himself. 
The deputy didn’t hear him. “Stephen, why did you come here?”

Stephen Cordova awoke between rows of apple trees, laid on the ground, looking to the rippling sky, which seamed and shattered with light before him. He saw Orion, but behind it, Orion’s hide and its cloud and the long lasso tying its joints together. He saw a familiar blob, though he wasn’t really sure where he’d seen it before. He struggled to pull his eyes away, but once again, set his mind to walking along the gravel road to find his home, hours before an important meeting with a man from the communications office.
