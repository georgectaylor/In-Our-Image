title: Scavengers
date: 2026-09-14
---
Olivia was a deeply sadistic person. There was no other explanation. She had a pleasant smile, kind, deep eyes, and a watchful but soft expression. She lived a beautiful and fulfilling life, and she was commonly said to be a good person – it was just that she couldn't think much about any of it. Moving through her mind was much like moving through rooms in an unfamiliar house, and behind some doors were ideas perhaps too foreign to really be foreign at all. She wondered if they'd simply been inside her all along, only undiscovered. A door opened too quickly, without the right preparation, would cause the flesh of her hand to merge with the plaster and the wood and she would become part of the room itself, and if inside, she saw a sickly, shriveled gray figure digging into something laying in the corner, that must be her burden to bear and her creature to contain. If flies buzzed in wait for something to be unleashed on, she thought, she must simply stop feeding them, and keep the doors closed. At one point, she briefly became convinced that her boyfriend was a deeply evil person - for a time, she was sure of it - but this, of course, was unfair to him. This was the musing of an incomplete mind. So she set it aside. She loved him, after all. She determined that she must have been projecting. 

Some people are simply made for each other. Some people make it obvious that they’ll be together forever. Sometimes, the reason isn’t clear and the signal is fuzzy, but it’s certainly there. Sometimes, that reason is unexpected, or even wholly irrational. Sometimes, this is just the inevitable state. In this case, it was all three. Neither could really explain why they fit together, except that they just did. James loved to talk and Olivia loved to listen, and if they didn’t love it, anybody else would certainly think that they did. He’d been to Australia and Greece and South Africa. He had played college basketball and had four siblings. He had plenty to say, and he did say a lot. Olivia would, too, if she had the time or the prompting to think about it, but she rarely did. She’d been to Canada and Brazil and she played volleyball and ran track – this just didn’t come up much. They had been together for six months, and James hadn’t even worked his way through the stories of his first two years of college yet. It was taking a while, which was his style. In the meantime, Olivia let herself be satisfied with listening.

She was from Florida, a long way from Chicago, where they met. James was from a small town on the coast of Michigan, where he could see the city skyline from his house. It had been six years since he left his family and many of his friends in Malloy and gone to the city for college. It had been two years since he had planned to be back, but he had found a different life for himself. Still an ardent family man, he would always be back for the holidays. The two sometimes disagreed on what a holiday was. On New Year’s Eve, they settled the debate on a technicality: it may not be a holiday, but James’ family would still have a party, and by precedent, that was it. They squeezed into Olivia’s barely-functional car with hardly enough space for two people and, knees close to their stomachs, drove around the crest of the lakeshore. The urban landscape was painted hastily with heavy strokes, and only gave way to the carefully crafted winter countryside and the snow balanced on pine needles with surgical precision after an hour of fighting traffic.

Later, at a point where Chicago became a suggestion in the sky and Indiana seemed more permanent than it should, James ran out of stories. It was a force of nature in the negative – a rainstorm evaporating in an instant. Olivia realized that her attention had been drawn into a small, dense center for a long, long time, and she struggled to decide what to do with it when that point collapsed. This was a somewhat familiar struggle. She could feel empty around James at times, a feeling of something’s absence so strong it became an object itself, like the hollow walls of a small room – always in reach, but not decorated by much. She just couldn’t tell what was really missing. She strained to push the feeling off of her, wiping it out of her eyes so that she could see the sky again.

There was business to handle in the car before they arrived. She got around to it after a few minutes of numb silence. There were names to go over. Lots of names. Not all of them would stick, but she had to try. Mom and dad were Billie and Scott. The tall blonde friend was Michael. The short brunette one was Mark. She had embarrassed herself before and was looking for a second chance with a few of these people, so she encouraged him to keep listing them. James thought this was funnier than it really was. When he stopped thinking of names, she took him through the flashcards.

“So who’s that guy you played baseball with in high school?” she asked. He replied that this was Emanuel, but that he likes the name Manny better.

“And the one that wears that denim jacket?”
This was Miguel, who would be with his girlfriend, Delaney, from New York.

	“I thought she was from Boston.” 
They both realized they weren’t sure. They agreed to not mention it until they were.

	“And that guy that crashed your car in high school?”
He reminded her that there was more to that guy than just the one time he crashed his car. But, yes, that was Jonny, and he did crash James’ car. She just can’t mention that she knows about that. It’s a sore memory.

	“Both of your parents are going to be there?”
They would, but they’d be asleep early. No siblings, he added.

	Olivia was growing more confident in her memory of the names. She knew that she wasn’t done, but this was the core group of her mind, the people she had one strike with already, plus the parents, who she had barely talked to but had always been fond of. They had met in Chicago, too, in the same neighborhood. When James asked how she knew that, she found that she couldn’t remember.

	Indiana disappeared, too, and they were in Michigan. Olivia’s eyes crossed over the tundra-like landscape, lightly dusted with snow and covered in yellow, stubborn grass. The sun was preparing for a quick exit, so she took her bearings while she still could. She had a tradition. She would compare the nighttime trip to whatever she was doing on New Year’s Eve to the daytime return, and inspect for signs that anything had substantially changed, as if the earth had shed its old layers. She knew that there would be nothing of the sort, and that she’d only see the same things from the other side. She looked anyway. 

The air was impressively calm. It demanded her attention for a moment. The trees were disappearing under a slowly rising sea that lapped at their roots and came with a flattering, deep orange hue. It was sure to be better than the return trip, which she would do tired, and with the sun in her eyes. That would be the real difference. It would be much more lonely then. 

	She had had something on her mind for a few days. It was one of James’ favorite stories, but she hadn’t given it the proper weight. It was a feature of its own in the landscape: a bungled, but still unsightly, murder, that had taken place in Malloy ten or twenty years before. A boyfriend or a husband killed his girlfriend or his wife or something like that for insurance or for jealousy or something else. It was either a stabbing or a shooting or a poisoning. Again, she was unsure. But it was gruesome. The feature that really stuck was the fact that she remembered nothing in at all. It was a template story of a man doing something rash and violent for a fuzzy and unclear reason, a story so outstanding but simple that it becomes the foremost thought for tourists who see the town’s name on a sign, or, of course, for people whose partners are from there. It was hard to forget but easy to get wrong. It was nondescript but monstrous. It was utterly confusing. 

The sun disappeared entirely, fog entered, and the car moved carefully over the murky ocean layer covering the road. Thirty minutes from the destination. Too much time for silence. Too little time for deep, dark secrets. Right in the middle of the two, in the liminal space between real thoughts, was the murder. Olivia asked about it.

	“What was that story you told me about the guy and his girlfriend? At Thanksgiving, I think? In Malloy. A few years ago.” James was confused. He ran the description through his head and came up with nothing.
“Or maybe it was a husband and a wife. It was - what was it? - like a poisoning or something. Or maybe he hit her with his car.” He still had nothing, but looked surprised when he realized that she was describing a murder.
	“I’ve definitely heard you mention it a few times, I just can’t remember the details. But I feel like I should.” He was nervous. He asked her, not all jokingly, if she was going to bring this up at the party.
“No – I’m not, like, serious about it,” she said, a bit flustered. “I was just thinking about it recently and I realized I couldn’t remember much about it.” He was slow to a response. She beat him to it.
“I’m sorry, that was –” It was fine, he said. He just wished he could remember what she was talking about. His voice reverberated a bit too much and he spoke a bit too slowly. She didn’t think much of it. But she did continue thinking about that story.

	He had mentioned it many, many times before, like many other things. Something about how her mind worked could make the details of those stories fuzzier with repetition, but not necessarily in a bad way. She had a brain primed to hear stories with whim and excitement every time because every possible detail already laid before her, strung like lights across one room in her head set aside just for that one story, waiting only to be lit in the right order. She was disappointed that some people thought that she didn’t care about them because she couldn’t hold onto some details. On her better days, she so loved learning about things that it was sometimes mistaken for something else. So, a murder with twists and turns and blood and guts and some important other details was just stored just as a murder, and she ached to listen to James tell it again. For this, she might come off as lacking the attention or the instincts to know how murders happen, but it already had space in her mind – it was only that the lights were flipped off and the room was dull and empty.

In a place like James’, where the living room was damp and hot even in the dry cold of November, this characteristic certainly did not read like intrigue. There, it read like surrender. There, James assumed that her mind was his to occupy with narratives and histories and his parents egged him on and contributed details that he had forgotten about their ski trips. He thought that one day, she would be fluent in the history of him, but she just wanted to talk to him, whatever that looked like, and she learned long ago that that meant letting him drone on with his stories. Olivia continued to dig for the details in her memory. She found questions, brief narrative strings, sure, but these were inside jokes with other people, stories told to the wrong audience, jokes in the wrong language. She found nothing worth asking about again.

	It was unsettlingly early. Far too early for the sun to be down. She would never get used to that, no matter how many winters she lived through. She would often lose the sense of familiarity with the moon and the stars she felt at the beginning of each night, and they would grow overwhelming and oppressive in their own way and she would miss the daytime again. But she would give them their chance every night. She would always greet them and converse for a moment. Suddenly, she found some notion, although a weak one, of how the story went.

	“Nighttime! It was nighttime, and they were stargazing.” For a second, he didn’t realize what she was talking about. She was oddly, inappropriately excited – she had figured it out. She put together the pieces as if she were the one telling him and he was the one at attention. Really, she was narrating to herself. “I still don’t remember what happened, but they were on the lake and they were stargazing.” James wasn’t as interested this time. He continued to make a show of thinking, but made no real effort to find anything. He wished that she would drop it. She didn’t notice. The stars did not like being mentioned this way, either, and when she looked back at them, she felt that this was somehow still intolerably incomplete and that they demanded that she try again.

“It must’ve been something else, though,” she mumbled. By now, she was thoroughly talking only to herself, pulling the strings of a rough idea taught into shape with the same half-attention she paid to her hands moving the wheel. She had noticed before that she was often putting together stories with no reception from James despite being right next to him, fighting against a heavy, awkward silence and continuing to trudge forward, but this was different. She found that she was really just speaking to herself, and that it was far easier and more productive. “It was just a control thing,” she continued, “and it was wintertime. Not Thanksgiving. He missed his chance on Thanksgiving because his parents were there.” She had let go, for a few moments, of the strange, indescribable feeling of forgetting something she should remember because it had been so long since she’d talked about her own life, or second-guessing whether a formative experience of hers was worth a mention at all. That feeling, that deep, cold rush to the chest of getting a lively silence in response to a funny story, for a brief time, became very distant. Then the flow stopped. Her presence of mind began to evaporate and her hands lightened on the wheel. James, she saw out of the corner of her eye, came across a more stern and concentrated look, and had slid his seatbelt on. She wondered why he hadn’t put it on before. She wondered why she could feel his thoughts, this strange discontent, somewhere in her head, though he stayed silent the whole while.

James turned towards her. He extended an arm from his passenger’s side seat, elbow straight and palm flat, to just above her head, only inches before it. In that dark, mildewy corner, his hand broached an unusual and expansive darkness, pushing it through his fingers as if he were moving through water. In an instant, like a lighting bolt illuminating the sky, the darkness dropped from his arm over her eyes, and his hand followed, swiping downwards to pull a dense curtain over her, depriving her entirely of light. In an instant, he had woven the tiny space they shared into a single, delicate sheet, and torn it in half. She had flinched at even his proximity, warned far in advance by some voice deep inside her which she did not recognize and did not know how to respond to. She became trapped regardless, trying to dodge the assault of the current as she was sinking to the riverbed. She felt nothingness, again as if it were its own tangible, heavy thing.

	She found the stars first when she began to wake. Her senses returned slowly, creeping over her skin with no urgency and bringing with them a feeling of brutal cold, but her vision kept her calm. The stars were still there. Before she could understand what was happening, she understood this, and she held onto it closely. When she saw James, she realized, noticing that he was standing, that she was on the ground. She rolled her head from side to side, seeing only forest, its details unintelligible through the haze in her eyes. Lifting her neck, she discovered that while her back lay on bare ground, her legs were tucked underneath the door of her car, extended awkwardly through the window opening, which had collapsed entirely against the ground. After vision, the second sense she regained was an enormous pressure on her legs. She squinted and blinked urgently as she looked back to James, but formed neither a good picture of him nor the strength in her chest to speak. 

As Olivia scanned the horizon, she noticed a faint glint in his eyes for a moment. She got the feeling that he was looking at her. She found that she was right, somehow, when she began to hear his voice, though his lips did not move. He told her that she was being irrational and should have let it go. She noticed that his voice often appeared like this – that her senses had much less to do with her interactions with him than did something else in her head. Olivia saw, in the deep caverns of his eyes, that sickly gray figure, swarmed by flies, its eyebrows made by finger-like bones that originated somewhere under the thin skin on the back of its head, wretch and turn as it left from its hideaway and looked directly at her. She did not know if closing the door would help.

She came to see that, somehow, they were not much different. Where she kept rooms in her head for the people in her life to stash their trinkets and belongings, any little reminder of them, he had an idea similar in concept. She came to understand that James had a room set aside for her in his head. It was a guest room, a distant, weak suggestion in the mind’s usual routine of the day, kept pristine in case it was needed. There, he could keep her close and comfortable and well-entertained. But she would still be only a visitor. It would never be hers. And he could forget her there entirely if he so felt like it, keeping a fragment of her soul only large enough to fit in that room and small enough still to throw away. He had grown upset that she had trashed the closet and picked the locks when she had stayed there before. He didn’t like that she had walked down the hall and heard him plotting to himself behind another door. He was going to seal her in forever to ensure that it wouldn’t happen again. He would have all or none of her. He dug around in his pocket. Olivia’s senses came together in a weak chorus to tell her that there was gasoline on the ground and that James held a lighter in his hand.

Desperation can come in small pieces, and it does quite often. It accompanies boredom and discomfort and anger and any mix of those. Olivia now felt a desperation unadulterated by anything else, indescribable by any analogy, purer than any she had felt before. It was desperation with no strings. It was a piece of her fighting with all its might to be felt.

James looked at her and she looked at him. She found that his grip on her worked both ways. She had the notion that she would be the first to see inside the mind of the murderer and the mind of the victim at once. This thought slid away into darkness, dismissed immediately. She found another thought shortly after, and this one seemed to stick better than the one before it. This wouldn’t work.

What wouldn’t work?

He wouldn’t get away with it.

	It was simple. He should've seen it before. His phone was on and it was pinging the cell tower. His location showed him right there, right then. He had left his fingerprints on plastic parts of the door and the console that were flung out of the car as it rolled, and while he would not find them now, investigators would find them in the morning. Upside-down handprints by the trunk would show that he had leaned on the car while it was overturned. The man at the gas station he would walk to to call a cab would see that he was bleeding. The cabby would notice that he smelled like gasoline and would charge him to have the back seats cleaned. His mom already knew that they had been in that car together because Olivia had texted her, so his story that she was driving up alone before he went up to meet them would not hold. The first police officer he would talk to, he would lie to, and he would get caught, because he would not be able to help himself. He knew this but could not see it. She had also known for a long time – only, she could see it now.

He was bold. He was dedicated. Sure, but he was not intelligent. Drunk on power or absorbed by an echo chamber, he had forgotten how things work outside of his own head. Emotions splattered together into a charred, brown mess. Olivia felt relief and anger and jealousy. She felt disappointment creep in sometime later. She acknowledged that he felt angry and vindictive for a reason she couldn’t yet feel with confidence, and she made him understand that this would be the end of the line for him – unless he fixed it. His phone was in his pocket. He could call for help. She could say what she wanted, but it would still be written up as an accident, no matter what, as long as he said that it was her fault. She would make it even easier, she proposed, and not say anything at all.

	He did not respond. He stared on for a while. But the hue of things changed, and the shadows from the other side of the curtain subsided. The embers Olivia saw floating off of the car died away and the apparitions of men in white plastic coveralls around her disappeared. James balanced gently along the tightrope between them, sitting entirely, supernaturally still, and all vibrations in it died. Ultimately, he did retrieve his phone, and called for an ambulance. A truce was inked when Olivia was pulled from the wreckage and lifted onto a stretcher. In the back of an ambulance, the narrow, bright blue walls and the technicians made her a room more homely and private than she’d had in months, and more private than they knew. Here, she felt safe to think freely. 

She realized that she did know perfectly well how murders happen, better than most others – a part of her own brain had been used to plan her own death. Still, when she had the thought of killing James, she could not tell if it was hers or his. But she knew all about how it would happen.
