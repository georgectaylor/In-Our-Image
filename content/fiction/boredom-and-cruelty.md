---
title: Boredom and Cruelty
date: 2026-09-14
---
Charles Wylock was a deaf man from his birth sometime in the 1950s until June of 2012, when, surrounded by family at the kitchen table of his North Carolina home, within view of the port he was delivered to from Scotland 60 years before, he tried on a hearing aid. A long, winding line of his adult children and their spouses shared expectant whispers, the last words they would ever organize about him he wouldn't be able to hear. They debated until the last second what the first question should be, and how they'd know if he enjoyed seeing new light. Somebody suggested singing him a song.

Charles’ true reaction to this moment, and the twenty seconds of uneasy static for which he wore it, would be long debated, but never settled. The leading theory is that he was absolutely repulsed – that human buzzing, even if contained to whispers, made him sick. So he threw it back in the box and simply went on without the thing. He was beloved beyond words, but he was always a strange and private man, so he was allowed to live beyond words. He made no mention of the experience ever again, and none was made to him. And he was again almost completely deaf, only ever really attentive to the window over the ocean, where he watched the seagulls swirl with the winds that made the waves dance. Teddy Wylock had watched his grandfather meet the prophetic experience of hearing and discard it, like Prometheus dropping a hot coal, when he was 20. He, too, never really knew why. 

On an overcast day in 2029, on the jagged teeth of the North Carolina coast, Teddy sat below angry clouds that seemed to nevertheless bend around him respectfully, holding off their torrents until he gathered the strength to go back inside. He had perched himself atop the generous downpour of the sandy hill that gave way into the sea some distance below him and the short green sprouts that marked successive zones of growing danger. He sat along the same long slope down to the water that was once visible out of his grandfather’s window, though not much was the same. The house was long gone, blown over, remembered in shards dug into the ground. Beside it was a town-sized white canvas tent and a parade line moving stretchers from a naval vessel in the port onto beds under its cover.

Teddy’s ears rang incessantly, and a scar across his left cheek beat against stitches to remind him that it was still there. He watched the waves pick at the sand, climbing higher and higher each time, for several minutes, until he realized that the sound he heard from them was not imagination. A seagull floating defiantly in the onslaught of cold air high above sounded a warning to him to get back under the cover of the tent, and it echoed in his head with no resistance. Scavenging mobs of them picked scraps from the edges of the humans' well-tread paths and called for more gifts, and when they could make time for it, they spoke to each other about whatever they had on their minds. They spun about each other happily, a carousel of white feathers, on the same beach, but many planets removed from the human carnage mere steps away. The ocean still lived, though all else was shrunk down into whispers.

Teddy no longer heard a thing of sirens and engines and machinery and human chatter. Voices passed straight through him. But he never stopped hearing the seagulls calling him back to the shore. They seemed to know that he could still hear them, he thought, and he imagined that they debated how to get a suitable signal from him that he was listening – that they whispered to each other about what song to sing him, as the family had once done with Charles.
