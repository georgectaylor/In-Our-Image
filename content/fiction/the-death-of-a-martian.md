---
title: The Death of a Martian
date: 2026-09-14
---
Malik enjoyed the feeling of being a busy man. Hardly ever was this the true state of things. He was an engineer who did little engineering, long ago appointed the leader of his team, and never since then in charge of much that required his own hands or his oversight. Beneath him, and entirely aside from him, his team constructed a beast that he considered his own anyway and which he took long, wistful walks through. But Malik was reminded at times that he was not the boss of all he could see, and that there were still, frustratingly enough, people he had to answer to. One of these men visited on a Friday while Malik was enjoying the feeling of work tapering off and noise subsiding as the beast had come together almost entirely. The man insisted on a conversation, and insisted that it happen in the conference room in the stomach of this beast.

The man was Travis, the senior liaison to the project from the Department of Defense, who visited rarely and saw little. The beast was a spaceship inside a hangar in Ohio, its face a mosaic of brilliant white and orange panels and one massive, sleek, rather imposing screen between the control room and the drab ceiling, which would soon turn on and disguise itself as a window to the cosmos outside. Really, the ship would never leave Ohio – and it didn't need to. The hangar was several hundred feet in length along every dimension and the ship inside was affixed to hydraulics and tracks that could make it twist and turn and shake violently during takeoff. Its compartments already held real boxes of nonperishable food tucked away neatly beneath the floorboards and inside the walls and in the ceilings. Scattered compartments, each affixed with their own pumps and shutoff valves to prevent the spread of bacteria and connected to the same air filtration system, held clean water, ionized to match standard earth levels. They were kept cool, but separated by vacuum seals from the ship so that they wouldn't collect condensation and ruin the humidity. Every drop used in the shower, the toilet, or the kitchen, or drank by the explorers and recollected from their breaths would be recycled and returned to one of six compartments. The lights were synchronized carefully along a full gradient of radiation to perfectly simulate the experience of a man on the ground in Ohio, which would keep the passengers sane, healthy, and in the rhythm of a natural routine. The cycle of the lights and temperatures would last two years, and end, as the passengers were told, when they landed on Mars.

The central conference room - also the dining room, the card room, and the video game room - was completed first. This room held the shape and the feeling of a cave, with the impressive, somehow complex emptiness and unique personality, as if it had always been here and had always been this empty, and was this way for some ancient purpose having only vaguely do with human habitation and much more to do with unseen currents. It was only to be visited and never to be tamed, showing itself only to those lucky enough to live after the water had subsided. But really, the whole thing was shocking to the senses.

The beast required entirely fresh inventions, proprietary, unseen technology, in quite a few areas. Most things in it were completely new contraptions. Brand new and barely conceivable, it was more real to the touch than it was in the mind. The beast was an achievement in two ways: technologically formidable, and psychologically compelling. Even when only a husk of the finished thing, it was shaping up to be a living monument to innovation, a home that didn’t dare make itself so warm that it stopped inspiring awe. In the middle of warmth and awe sat the realization that nobody really understood what it was for. The technology would certainly work for any genuine sustained space travel in earth-simulating conditions, but had no such use yet. Some technology, like the control room screen, would only work for deception and little else, if anything else at all. But those who spent only the workday in it, and knew that it was really on the ground, were happy to think only as much about it as they were paid to, and did not have to see it through the lens that it was their ark into the unknown and that it was inescapable. They did not really see it with that engineering unadulterated awe. So certain things went unnoticed and unthought.

 The conference room was, at the moment, most like an office. Blueprints decorated the table atop scattered miscellaneous paperwork so that Malik could take visitors in the room and impress them with the sheer detail of the job. Travis, for a change, was unimpressed. As they settled into their seats, Malik’s sleek smile faded and his eyes narrowed behind his sunglasses, which he wore specifically to annoy Travis. He took them off and placed them in the breast pocket of his off-code polo shirt, which Travis also despised, but said nothing of. On the topic of Malik’s dress habits, they agreed perfectly – it was sacrilegious. But only one of them was winning this strange battle over it, and it was the man who gave all he had to the cause of ruining his own appearance and humiliating the project’s public image.

“What can I do for you?” he started.

Travis always carried a briefcase, which he now lifted off of his lap and placed on the table. He removed three papers, which he slid across the table to Malik and spread out before him, in chronological order. “Have you seen these?” he asked. 

Malik looked at them closely for a minute, but didn’t read them. “I’m sure I have,” he responded, “but I see a lot of memos.” 

Travis leaned across the table and pointed to underlined portions at the top of each paper, which read “Response requested” and listed Malik, among other names. “You were meant to confirm that you’d read them. I haven’t heard back from you yet.” 

Malik shrugged. “Well, I’m sorry if I didn’t get back to you on time, but I didn’t realize it was that urgent.” Travis again looked unentertained. He pointed again to a line along the top of the last memo, which read “Urgent.” He looked forward intently, waiting for a response. Malik sighed dramatically.

“Look, Travis” - he tried to be careful to not come across as disrespectful - “I’m sorry I didn’t respond to these, but I know you didn’t come here just to follow up on some memos, so why don’t you just read them to me like I’m sure you will anyway?” It didn’t work.

Travis had little reaction to what clearly was disrespectful because he had already planned out this interaction in his head. He took the memos back and returned them to his briefcase, which Malik thought looked suspiciously empty besides just those items, making him wonder if he had brought it out just for this. “Psych has a new strategy,” Travis said, “and it’ll require a little logistical work.”

“Psych?” Malik asked in return.

“The Psychology Committee.” Travis knew that Malik would be unfamiliar, but took this as his opportunity to kick him around a bit. “Mark leads that committee. You met Mark at the Christmas gathering, I'm sure you remember him.”

Malik was uninterested. “What’s their concern? If there’s a committee involved, I know for sure their deadline passed. I got all the prints three months ago.” He also knew that he had not been to any recent Christmas gathering, whatever that meant.

Travis knew that he was right, but didn’t want to get into it with him about why it didn’t matter. In truth, the Psychology Committee was new, existent as of only two weeks before. They were new hires at Defense, refugees from the rules and strata of the other federal departments, but Travis did not know for sure whether they were poached or hired willingly. That, too, wouldn’t matter to their jobs, so he definitely wouldn’t tell Malik about it. 

“It’s a new order. Remember, though, I’m just the messenger,” Travis said. He pointed out a finger as if it kept the other man at a distance. This he was, but he hid how excited he was to force some change out of this crew, which he resented he had not gotten to put together, or he certainly would not have chosen to make Malik so important. He opened the briefcase again, and from a hidden inner lining along the top, removed a blueprint, and placed it on the table. The paper was occupied to a dominant extent by an illustration of a rectangular box, shown by arrows to be built into the wall in the medical bay, with a more cavernous space below. Travis reached in again and produced another blueprint, which showed an expansive vent system, like the arms of an octopus, poised quietly and ready to strike, connecting to an output beside each crew member’s bed. The head of the octopus, to which these vents connected, was a cluster of gas canisters in a cage on the ground outside the ship. The first memo Malik had missed was to introduce these two changes and the final two were descriptions of those plans. Warning, though, might not have helped.

His breathing grew shallow and the blood in his head retreated into his chest and his hands, which became heavy and sluggish with the pressure. His fists clenched and became numb, his arms became oppressively heavy, and his breath stung his throat with an unbearable heat and strong resistance, as if he were breathing in thick smoke.

He rose fearfully from his seat, attempting to distance himself from the blueprints by extending his hands outward in rebuke. “That’s not happening,” he said sternly, standing on no solid ground at all. “I refuse that. I absolutely refuse that.” 

Travis stood up as well, and pointed back to the chair. “Sit back down,” he shot back.

Malik stayed upright for a second and returned to his seat. “That will push us back by weeks. At least.”

“Six days. We have the prints already and Auxiliary gave us an estimate. They have the materials coming tomorrow.” 

“Auxiliary?” Malik briefly browsed his memory for a clue to what this meant. When it came to him, it brought little relief with it. “You’re kidding me. Not Auxiliary.” The Auxiliary Committee was the bone thrown by Defense to the Department of Energy, a secondary collaborator to the project that built the beast, which had, until this point, only been meant to assure the other departments that their money was going to good use and that progress was going smoothly. Auxiliary’s only accepted function, the only one they were prepared for, was stepping in if a timeline was in peril. “That’s not their purpose either, Travis, and you know that. How do we know they’re even in the country?”

“I know they’re in the country because I saw Frank yesterday. He made these prints. He approved the timeline. He’s the one who convinced Energy to put them all up in apartments to keep them nearby in case they’re needed, and now, clearly, they’re needed.” Travis saw Malik’s eyes narrow into a pure anger, which he knew he was unlikely to be really capable of. He knew that Malik didn't know who Frank was. Travis kept explaining. “They’re meant for this, Malik. It’s not an insult. The timeline changed, and Energy needs to put their dogs on it.”

“So did Energy put together this little stunt with the Psychology Council?” Malik responded.

“The Psychology Committee,” Travis corrected.

“Whatever. Did they do this? Because I can’t imagine they want to use their budget to let Defense push another pet project through.”

“They have no choice,” Travis said, “and neither do you. You and your team will show them the ropes on Monday and then you’ll cordially ask them what they need and then you’ll stay out of the way. And when they’re done six days later, you’ll return. They’re working through Saturday. They’ll work nights if they need to. When Frank says six days, he means six days.” Malik became quiet. They stared at each other for a moment. 

Malik took a deep breath, held it for a second, and replied. “You know, they did that to me, too. That little Schroedinger’s box. The disappearing trick.” He spit the words out with uncomfortable force at the end of each sentence. He was not used to yelling, so he fell back to what he thought was an intimidating demeanor of speaking. He let Travis stew in silence for a bit. It didn't do much. “They thought I was dead, Travis. I might as well have been, too, because I was asleep for six hours and woke up sweaty and cold in Philadelphia. Why did they send me to Philadelphia? I didn’t get back to the crew for a week, and they thought I was dead the whole time. That was all on purpose.”

Travis laid his hands on the table in a gesture of surrender. “I know, Malik, but you didn’t have the Psychology Committee.”

Malik shot out of his seat again and paced angrily behind his chair. “That’s not what I meant. There’s no way another Committee would’ve helped. Do you really know the whole story about what happened?” He glared at Travis, whose face stayed cold. It betrayed that he didn’t care much about the story. But Malik did. “They simulated a biological attack at the listening station and put us on lockdown for a week. They brought that Army truck to the bay and they left it there and then they gassed me in my sleep.” Travis remained unmoved, even after being left to stir again. “They let my men load me into the truck and then drove me away. And my men kept working. And you people let them think that it was their fault.” Malik now spoke intentionally and slowly, like moving along a thin line in the sand. But he stopped talking, because his voice was dampening and his throat was swelling, and Travis was still his boss.


Travis knew, because it was his job to know, that Malik was a brilliant man. He had post-graduate degrees in physics and specialized in fluid dynamics and acoustics. He had commanded listening stations, radar installations, and signal intelligence operations for years. In a personal, more historical sense, Travis did not know Malik’s story, but had heard scraps of it, reverberations through the wall, which Psych told him with little enthusiasm. He had heard some business about anthrax and some elements of the earliest idea for an embedded, fully-empowered Psychology department in Defense development. He had heard that Malik let anybody who would listen, and many who wouldn't, know about his frustration with the path Defense research was taking.

He knew that Malik’s death and revival were the start of a whole new paradigm in military research, and that, even more so than if he had really died, Malik gave his life to his nation and its new dreams by falling asleep for a few hours. Malik’s heart was already in this work, even if he didn't feel that it was. Travis knew that his story solidified forever the philosophy that, for people to truly live frugally and in total focus, for whatever purpose, they must consider lives and empathy to be resources to be rationed, as well – they must be taught to accept strange deaths without question or second thought, mourn briefly, and then keep working. So Travis didn't try hard to make things easier for the man he reluctantly saw as his most promising subject. He trusted that Malik would discover his importance eventually, even if his work was half-hearted at best now, even if he missed what was obviously coming of this project, even if he could only see the results in retrospect. He knew that Malik was building an army and that his example would lead their hearts and minds bravely into tempests he could not even imagine. He felt strongly that even an unknowing king holds a king’s responsibilities all the same.

 “I know,” he said. “They briefed me on that.” They had not – he had been told in less than a full sentence over coffee with a coworker more than a week ago, and this was all he had heard until just now. “Psychology went over your case specifically, and they saw what it did to those men. They want to be sure it won’t happen again, and that shows, to me, that they understand what happened and what it meant.” Malik looked unconvinced. “This model is far better. Nobody will think it’s their fault. After this crew loads a body into the –” 

Malik interjected. “Not a body. They aren't bodies, Travis.”

Travis continued without reacting, talking over him a bit. “After the crew loads the incapacitated crew member into the med bay disposal unit, the manual will tell them to radio Command, who will radio back and say that the cause was a genetic disease. They’ll say that it went unnoticed in evaluation and that they’ve just investigated again and found it. And the crew will believe it. They'll have to. It won’t be the same.”

Malik sat back down and brought his hands over his eyes as a headache spread across his temples. He wanted to ask what came next – would they be distracted with a solar flare? Would they be pulled away from a funeral service by a fluid leak somewhere? Would Command be watching to ensure that they timed it right? In Defense, genetic disease was an especially touchy subject – would they take the crew member’s family away, too, just for the realism?

“This is why we have Auxiliary on it,” Travis said. “They haven’t dealt with this before. They don't have this conflict. And we’re prepared to send you on R&R during the time they’re working.” This was another addition of Travis’ own volition. He had no approval to offer a Rest and Recuperation excursion. But when he felt the strange, cold bite of sympathy dig into his hands, he pulled away immediately and put his mind away from it, leaving the offer before Malik.

He was unsure if the conversation continued after this point. If it had, he gathered nothing from it. Travis got up with his blueprints and left at some point, and Malik was again left alone, with a small scrap of blueprint penned with a number to call to request time off. Suddenly, he didn't feel much like getting very far from the ship. Without being said out loud, a message instead hidden somewhere in the breast pocket of Travis’ pristine suit or in the tone of his voice told Malik that his soul was tied inevitably to the inner chambers of this strange, large contraption, and that he would be here in spirit with whatever crew was about to be loaded into it, no matter what he did in flesh. In the single true formative moment of his career, where he felt the closest he had to his work since waking up in the bed of that truck, he found, as if he really were a part of it, that machines like this were too big to fail and that some souls got stuck in their gears. He wondered whether this ship was really yet that big and whether he might still find space for a weakness in it before it carried his soul somewhere he didn't want to go.
