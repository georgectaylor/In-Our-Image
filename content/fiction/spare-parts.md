---
title: Spare Parts
date: 2026-09-14
---
As Sophie guided her brother down the stairs and to the edge of the woods, where the grass gave way to the thick brush of oak trees, she thought about the things she’d left unspoken about the wilderness behind the family home.

Their house sat at the end of three miles of road through pristine wilderness and at the peak of a large hill, as if guarding the threshold to something bigger hidden behind it. It held a stature like a lighthouse, a sentry against the seasonal changes, a dagger holding fabric into the ground from which the trees grew. It was tellingly multi-generational, a great amber block preserving years of memories side by side as easily as coats fit together in a closet, where every gathering recalled one thirty years before and every picture hanging on the walls showed somebody still alive, at least in part.

 Rudy, the oldest, had not ventured outside these walls for many months. Now, to challenge his easy routine of introversion, the hot, dense smell of the summer was returning, lifting from the soil strong, lucid memories which glid on the ripples of hot, rising air. This was the same smell that had begun to dissipate as the sun began to soften before autumn almost a year before, when he had crawled away from his overturned car and out into the dim moonlight along the highway, and became comfortable with simply staying inside.

As the ground warmed and dew began to glisten on the leaves, his mother decided that it was time for Rudy to get outside. She had said this many times before, and understood that she would never really win this battle. But Rudy became convinced after a time that it was his idea and thought that he could simply let his mom feel like she had won. Whose idea it really was would never be decided with certainty, which was probably best left as it was – those debates could continue for days. With Sophie anchoring him from under his arm, he took practice laps around the kitchen until his feet remembered the feeling of the ground pushing back and his heart began to calm its fluttering.

When Rudy described his family situation to others, they would often assume that Sophie was an older sister. Truthfully, their father had left the task of caring for then thirteen-year-old Rudy and their mother to the then nine-year-old baby of the family. Sophie had once stayed up all night to rearrange the furniture in her mother’s room to surprise her when their dad was in the hospital and on several occasions had made strides to domesticate wild animals. This made their mother worried, but impressed their father enough to declare the girl in the princess crown the matriarch of the family and the pets from their outdoor domain.

Now, Sophie was all of thirteen years old and was taking her continuing responsibilities very seriously. In the morning, she would wake up at seven, open all the blinds downstairs, put on a kettle of tea, set out kibble for the stray cats, and make her lunch. Then, if nobody else was up by the time she was done, she would sing, dance, and loudly play the grand piano until they were. She made sure that the fridge was stocked, all three were fed, and that everybody made it to school on time. When Rudy had still been in the habit of going out late, she would leave the door unlocked for him, and when he smoked in his room, she would prop open the windows. When he talked about his situation, he struggled to get across that every story about him was really a story about his sister. When he was under the searing lights of his hospital room, he laughed a bit, in spite of his surroundings, as he told a nurse that she, then only twelve years old, was his emergency contact.

Sophie kept secrets well. It was part of this job, and she had been doing it for a while. She hadn’t acknowledged the long walks through the woods beyond the backyard she had been taking. The truth, the part she wouldn’t tell her mom to avoid making her sad, was that she sometimes felt that she could talk to people who weren’t there when she went far enough down the trail – people like their father. She realized after a time that what she was doing was not really talking and that nobody was really listening. She had a sort of enhanced privacy here, the privilege to not be seen or heard at all, so little communication happened. More realistically, she would stand still enough that she completed a circuit with the ground and the heartbeat of the forest became hers. Here, she would begin to see a map of the grounds on her palms and hear familiar voices echo in the bird calls, and the souls splitting between the branches in the trees, mere memories gliding through human thoughts, would become tangible as projections to the senses. This was close enough for Sophie.
 
She had once told Rudy about this experience, this interface directly onto the soul of the land, while they sat on the back porch as the sun went down. She struggled to put it into words, and ultimately felt that she had fumbled an accurate description. As he watched the dusk settle through the trees he resolved that he would one day make it out there to talk to him, too. Rudy wanted to tell his dad how beautiful the sunset looked and how big Sophie was getting and that their mom was doing well, even if he already knew, and even if he was just pretending to be heard. Sophie had wondered whether it was right to use that moment to tell him about the other things she’d seen and felt there, too, and decided against it.

Today, Rudy felt the grass under his feet for the first time in months. It caved gently as if welcoming him back, like an awkward hug with slumped shoulders, and dew crept over the toe of his shoe. He held Sophie’s hand tightly as they made their way down the gentle slope to encounter the trees, and they stood before the edge of the woods for a moment. Both of them expected the other to say something. Neither did. Rudy let his hand slip from his sister’s and took the step over the boundary into the woods. 

He felt moss beneath his feet and breathed in the rich, layered smells of decaying wood and mycelium in the air. Sophie followed behind him as he began to walk in stride, paying each step an exhaustive level of attention so that he wouldn’t fall. He wouldn’t admit that he was scared. Sophie wouldn’t admit that she could see it. The girl watched carefully over her brother as he began to rediscover the path he had walked through the trees as a child. It was usually beaten and obvious, but after those months of lighter use, it had been conquered again by vines and a patchwork of moss. Rudy’s gait began to strengthen, and he earned more confidence with each step.

Sophie sometimes felt that superstition wasn’t a choice, but something that came when you let it. She had noticed her father’s habit of touching the edge of the counter before he drove in the car and how every time he saw a cardinal, he would wave to it. Sophie knew that he didn’t expect anything from doing these things; her father was, deep down, a realist. More than anything, he did them for the sake of trying. He knew it made his wife nervous when he would drive at night or in the rain, and he knew how much Sophie loved pointing out cardinals from the kitchen window. So every time he left the house, he would make sure his wife saw him touch the edge of the counter, and every time they spotted a cardinal together, he would tell Sophie to greet it. It was the futility of it that gave these gestures their value, she finally had begun to realize. He thought that life was a gift you give yourself. Even if nothing would come of it, even if he made himself thirty seconds later by running back into the house to do his little ritual, he would do so happily. His life was the sum of the promises he made himself, even the ones that didn’t come true. As she was preparing to reveal, she had come about this realization a bit late.

Rudy continued to walk with more and more confidence. Their surroundings began to shift in color, and a gentle green hue took over the air as the decaying light of the sun filtered through the canopy of leaves. They were in the territory of tattered animal hides and chipped gold coins, arrowheads and buffalo horns. These woods held shrines to old gods and shipwrecks that laid in pieces on the forest floor. Here, the earth had sculpted a strong fortress with a beautiful cover, molded carefully to echo the chirps of the birds and the groans of the bending trees, and otherwise simply tossed its belongings away onto the ground there. Here were castles where warriors were born beside the mounds where they were buried; here were ships that carried humans for the first time deep into the Pacific and through the cover of the imposing, unimpeachable, cloudy night. In these woods, she saw stars in the sky that were long dead and a moon that had not yet received its cratered face. It was a sort of a hiding place for the things that were destined to be forgotten, a proof that destiny always ends somewhere. Sophie wondered if Rudy would believe his eyes if he’d seen these things, too.

Their father had taken here here before. This was the first element of the secret she hadn’t shared with her brother yet. This meant that they were not really exploring now, though she let Rudy think, as he walked the only available path, that he was in command. Really, this was a well-travelled, well-mapped ruin, and only two people in that house were familiar with it, which seemed to be one too many for the matter at hand. Rudy would usually stop at the point along this path where the sun or the stars would cut out, but Sophie and her father had usually gone further than that. He would then choose some point to gently nudge her back the other way. When Sophie first went out on her own, she found that if she went far enough, further than she’d gone with her father, there would be a clear change in the atmosphere. The feeling of passing this threshold didn’t dissipate after the first time. She began to feel it again now, walking with Rudy. She had once heard that the only way a secret could be kept between two people was if one of them was dead, but she found this secrecy unfulfilling and inclined to share it with another person.

As Rudy walked, his eyes stayed on the horizon; he did not notice the improvement in his stride or the straightening of his muscles as life shot through his body. He watched as the bugs filled the air with a gentle luminescence and their colors washed over the ground. The trees grew taller and wider as they went, their branches sinking to the ground and spreading across it in webs. The air felt warmer than it should be this time of day, and the sounds of birds he didn’t recognize filled the sky, moving with him like bodyguards. As the maze of individual trees gave way to a more solid and imposing wall of lush green, blending together to block out the setting sun. 

Rudy realized, strikingly, later than he should have, that he’d never been this far into the woods before. He wondered how long they’d been walking; he debated facing Sophie to suggest turning back, but he could still feel her close behind him, and decided to keep going as long as she was. Only a small part of him was still intent on taking a quick walk or exploring sentimentality, which their mother thought was the purpose. A much louder part was curious to explore the feeling of freedom that found him as he went. A growing part wanted to see his father. He did not know if his mother had known that this would be the direction he chose.

Life is a gift you give yourself, he had said. Sophie thought back to that and the other dad-isms she’d heard over the years. She would pull them out of the metaphorical cabinet in the back of her mind when she needed them, a tangible reminder of his personality and his voice, an impression that seemed to grow even stronger over the years. Maybe this was because her most formative years were underpinned by grief, but even if this was considered, the larger role was still played simply by her dad’s remarkable sticking quality. Every story told and every memory kept about him came in full color, lucid and rich and comprehensive to the senses. He would want her to keep going, she often thought to herself, and she was right. She imagined that he approved of what she’d been doing to help Rudy get better, even if she couldn’t be sure. She knew that he had always wanted to take her deeper into the forest than he was allowed to by mom. She remembered the promises she’d made years ago to bring something cool back for the two who stayed in the house while she ventured through the woods on her father’s shoulders, calling out loudly to the birds and whispering discretely to the stars, looking out over the valley through the dancing leaves, asking them to give her something to gift back. It would take more looking, she knew then – the forest egged her on.

The sky began to look like heavy, sloppy brushstrokes on a vast canvas as the light of the stars started to fade. The constellations were replaced by flocks of fireflies that cast light in thin, falling strings over the forest floor like a scratchy blanket. The pendant in Sophie’s fist began to glow, which she could see, but ignored. Rudy was now walking confidently, no longer cautiously watching his steps as he had been before, as if he had been pulling himself through the motions with attached strings like a puppet. His legs seemed to follow his eyes, and his eyes were fixed forward.

 A crescendo among the swaying tree branches escalated to a roar, like the sound at the lip of a river one walks into blindly, relying on the roar entirely, and then cut away, as if they were now underwater. The pair stopped atop a sudden decline where they found space to stand side by side in the widening delta between the trees. They found something like the mouth of a crater in the earth, lined by a thick mess of vines. At the bottom, bones the length and width of a grown man’s whole body lay stuck prominently from the pale, sandy soil, forming the shape of a hollow rib cage. In several places among the ribs and along the spine, crevices of a slightly darker color were apparent, like cracks in an old log splitting down the middle. This was a monster story and its dramatic score frozen in time – the world’s oldest, most expressive statue. Rudy wondered how many times the thing’s bones had broken and filled themselves in, how many years of twisting and expanding it endured as it grew along these fault lines. A hill on either end formed, Rudy guessed, the hip bones and the skull, out of sight under the dirt and the grass, haphazardly sprinkled on. The grass had no problem growing, pushing itself out in long, brilliant strands, but the thing’s tenure in this spot must have been short enough, or the winds tame enough, that it had not yet been covered in any substantial amount of earth. The branches held themselves still, respectful and poised, in a dome over the body, and the only light available, sketching the edges of the bones against the backing dirt, was that of the curious fireflies.

Rudy ran wild guesses through his head as to what animal the thing belonged to, or what massive, beastly imitation of a familiar animal it could be. He thought only of one thing. He thought it must be a human. It looked as familiar as the strange, memetic habits of any typical day, reminding him just as much of himself as absently rubbing his eyes or itching his nose. This might be a fault of imagination or of discovery. Maybe it was an animal more like a true monster, foreign entirely to his own world, but he did not think this to be likely. He became afraid that he did understand the thing perfectly well.

Rudy thought of those moments adults sometimes talk about that contextualize one’s entire life. Rudy thought this must be it for him. The life and death of the creature these bones belonged to were matters lost to the ages, but he considered in broad terms the irony of a thing so long lost and so foreign to his eyes yet so well preserved for observation in this hole in the ground, as if it were granted this resting spot by the same architect that shaped these hills and dug the rivers, the same hand that killed it and let it be forgotten. It allowed this creature to die here alone, and then showed it the mercy of protecting it from the rain and the elements and giving it a home at the end of the long gradient merging the tangible and the intangible, at the end of a strange journey through the hazily-defined space in the woods. It was hard to understand the lonely fate of something as grand as the thing that lay before him and not consider what it reflected of himself.

This was where Sophie bore the responsibility, rather than just the itch, to tell Rudy why she had brought him here. She knew fully well what he was thinking, and she knew in exactly what order the cards fell. She knew how those thoughts would start to look if he went back home on his own tonight and slept on the experience, and she knew that he would wake up many times that night and look to the stars for reassurance that they were still there and were still untouchable. She knew that this had the power to make him feel unspeakably small and unthinkingly powerless. She knew that he would soon start wondering whether this had been their father’s true fixation in this forest, and whether he had thought the same things Rudy was thinking now. For both of them, the inevitable answer would be yes, and anybody who knew their father would know that this would be a very real fascination for him, and that he would of course make space for this in his ontology of the world, even if it were still sloppy and incomplete, even if this addition reversed his progress and confused him thoroughly.

With the long walk and the grand reveal finished, Sophie moved on before Rudy could fully process, because she already knew what his next thought would be. Prominently in their view, an unusual dark section  against the uneasy white, like a chip in a brilliant marble statue, a small section of one rib bone had been dug away. It would be minor, easily overlooked if noticed at all, to anybody else. But the three witnesses to this scene had been three minds in the same vein, three stars dancing through the sky on the same string. They all thought the same. One had made the indentation, and later, the other two had noticed it easily. Carved out of the softening bone before being encased in silver was the white center of Sophie’s teardrop-shaped pendant, a final, parting gift from their father, the jewel in the strange crown that was the end of his life.

The heaviest secret she held was the same burden her father had collected: a great dissonance between the governing philosophy and the ultimate discovery. Usually a realist with a penchant for superstition that remained just a penchant, a lifelong believer in art for art’s sake, their father had discovered, whether out of gross curiosity or outright desperation, that the bone fragment contained a soul. She did not know how long her father was meant to live, or how long he himself expected to, or how long he might have if he were exceptionally lucky - she would not dare to ask her mother any of these things - but she was certain that he was around longer than he thought he would be, and he kept the pendant on him often. It was always around his neck or in his breast pocket, always where its heat could reach his heart, and presumably kept it beating.

She struggled to rationally understand, and aimlessly flailed against the moral implications of, the difference between the life she intended to live and the life she really had. What she wanted to tell her brother was this: there was nothing about his life, despite how often he felt it, even if according to the right he had to feel it, that was shameful. Sophie could make no earnest attempt to make him feel better about it, nor could she fix it, as his muscles would become weak again and his joints would return to rust as soon as they were away from the forest. But that material world was hardly half of their reality. 

What her life really looked like was far different: she could keep this philosophy of stubborn encouragement and endless optimism, and she could guide him that way as long as he needed. But for that time, the time until he was well enough to venture out on his own, they would be swimming blindly through murky blue waters, Sophie just inches ahead of her brother, able to lead him only as long as he was in her wake. Her real burden was that she was unsure if she could ever move too far from the pendant for too long. Only shortly after her father’s death did she notice that her skin grew pale and her head grew heavy if it was too far away, and that she couldn’t remember the last time she saw her father alive without it near him. It was not reasonable to say surely, but perfectly reasonable to suspect that it was irremovable now.

 She was unsure whether the humans here controlled their fascinations or the other way around. She was unsure whether the forest was there for them at all, and whether it only used them as fodder, vehicles to spread its tightly-packed, vulnerable soul to other corners of the globe through desperate people. She did not know what her father had traded for life, but was not interested in any sort of trade at all, though she was reminded every time she felt the supernatural warmth of the pendant that the trade had been made for her. “Was it a gift? Or was it just left behind?” asked Rudy. She responded that she was entirely unsure. She hadn't thought about it much. She now understood the difference. If Rudy had pushed, she might have told him that she truly believed that her father would have gotten rid of it, or told her to stay away from it, if he were strong enough, or if he had the time. She supposed that this meant that it was no gift at all.

As they tread their way back, impressed once again, but this time with a far more dull reception, with the fully-guided tour through time and space, Rudy’s stride weakened and his knees and back again began to pain him. Walking in front, it was now him who had to lead confidently, despite his waning strength, and he did this valiantly. He stumbled into the clearing of the backyard, tired and defeated, but prideful – while it had been something strange and unfamiliar that stood him up, it was his own legs and their own power that had pushed him through, and he began to think about taking more walks and chasing the feeling further.

With her help climbing the rear stairs, Rudy and Sophie made their way to their seats on the back porch. They were both now thoroughly defeated by fatigue, their shoes too covered in mud to yet go inside, and they respected their mother’s habit of tidiness for and the purpose it served that they may still have been too young to fully understand. They looked out over the emerging stars, shyly revealing themselves as the sun fell away, and thought quietly, privately, but unavoidably in collaboration with each other. They wondered how they might hold onto their father in some other way besides carrying around a shred of him. They wondered what a fixation with death does to a living thing, and decided that they would rather not become personally familiar with the question. They would have to recognize the misleading lights when they came and turn to each other to remember what real life looked like. They wondered if they could ever get rid of the pendant.
